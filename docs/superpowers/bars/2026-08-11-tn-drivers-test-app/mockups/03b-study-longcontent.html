Safe driving · Work zones & move over

It is just after nine on a wet October evening on a two-lane state highway posted at 55 mph. You come over a rise and see, in this order: an orange diamond sign carrying a black flagger symbol, then a lane-closure taper marked out with drums, and then — stopped on the right shoulder just past the taper — a wrecker and a Tennessee Highway Patrol cruiser, both running their hazard flashers, with the cruiser's blue lights on as well. Oncoming traffic is steady, and the closure means the lane beside you is not available. What does Tennessee law require of you here?

This rule moved after the manual was written Tennessee widened the Move Over Law in 2023. We test the version in force today, not the 2022 text — the manual's wording is quoted below so you can see both. All three corrections

Look it up in the manual The whole passage — it contains the answer

“Tennessee's ‘Move Over Law' requires motorists to move over into the adjacent lane of traffic, if it is safe to do so, when approaching any authorized emergency vehicle, recovery vehicle, highway maintenance vehicle or utility service vehicle that is stopped on or beside the roadway with its emergency, warning or flashing lights in use. If a lane change is not possible, or cannot be made safely, the driver shall reduce the speed of the vehicle to a speed that is safe and prudent for the existing road, weather and traffic conditions, and shall be prepared to stop. This requirement applies on every highway in the state, whether it carries two lanes or more, and it applies by day and by night. Failure to move over or to slow down is a Class C misdemeanor; where the violation results in property damage, bodily injury or death, the offense and the penalties are substantially greater. Work zones carry their own duties in addition to this one: speeding fines in a marked work zone with workers present are doubled, and injuring a highway worker carries a mandatory court appearance.”

Tennessee Comprehensive Driver License Manual · Section: Sharing the Road with Others · p. 68
